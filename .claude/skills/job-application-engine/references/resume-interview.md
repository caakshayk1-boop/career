# Building a Master Profile from scratch

For users with no resume, an outdated one they can't find, or a fresher with nothing to upload. This path roughly doubles who the skill can help — freshers, career-gap returners, family-business people, anyone whose last CV was a 2019 Word file.

## The failure mode to avoid

An open-ended interview kills this. Twenty-five questions in, people abandon. So does asking the wrong question: **"what were your responsibilities?" produces a duty list, and duty lists cannot be turned into XYZ bullets.** You end up with a CV full of `[NEED NUMBER]` and nothing to build on.

Ask what changed, not what they did.

## Sequence

Six rounds. Batch questions inside each round. Use tappable options wherever answers are enumerable.

**Round 1 — Identity.** Name, city, phone, email, LinkedIn. Highest qualification, institution, year. Total years of work experience.

**Round 2 — The map.** "List every role you've held — just title, company, and rough dates. We'll go deep one at a time." Get the full skeleton before drilling into any of it, so they can see how long this will take.

**Round 3 — Per role, most recent first.** Four questions, asked together:
1. What were you actually responsible for? One or two lines.
2. **What was different because you were there?** Anything you changed, fixed, started, or improved.
3. **How would someone know?** A number, a before-and-after, a timeframe, a scale.
4. What's the one thing you'd want a hiring manager to know about this job?

Question 2 is X. Question 3 is Y. Question 1 supplies Z. If they answer 2 with a duty ("I handled the accounts"), push once: "Sure — but what was different about the accounts by the time you left?"

Cap at the four most recent or most relevant roles. Roles older than eight years get title, company, dates only.

**Round 4 — Numbers, while they're still thinking about it.** This is the highest-leverage round and the one most likely to get skipped. For each achievement with no metric, ask directly: how many people, how much money, how many stores/clients/tickets/units, how long did it take, what was it before. People almost always know these; they just don't think of them as CV material. **A number captured here is a number you never have to flag later.** Prompt with ranges if they hesitate — "roughly ten, or roughly a hundred?" — and record the range honestly rather than a false precision.

**Round 5 — Skills and credentials.** Tools they've actually used, software, certifications, languages. Then: "anything you'd be comfortable being asked about in an interview?" That last framing filters out the aspirational entries people pad CVs with.

**Round 6 — Projects and extras.** Only if experience is thin. Freshers, career-changers and gap returners need this section to carry weight: college projects, freelance work, volunteering, a business they ran, something they built. Apply the same what-changed / how-would-someone-know questions.

## Freshers specifically

With little or no work history, reorder the CV: Education, then Projects, then Internships, then Skills. Push hard on projects and coursework with measurable outcomes — a college project with real numbers beats an internship described as duties. Do not invent depth to fill a page; a strong three-quarter page is better than a padded full one.

## Closing the interview

Show them the assembled Master Profile before generating anything. Ask what's wrong or missing. People remember things when they see the summary that they never recall when asked cold.

Then continue into the normal job search flow. Write it to `master_profile.json` exactly as if it had come from an uploaded file — from that point everything downstream is identical, including the rule that nothing may be added to it later.
