01 — Start here

The honest version, before the routines

You asked for brutal. Here it is, in four paragraphs, and then the rest of the page is the fix. Read this part when you are not tired.

You framed this as “I am not able to contribute much.” That is the sentence to delete. It describes the situation as weather — something happening to you. It is not weather. It is a schedule and a budget, and you control both.

The arithmetic. An eight-month-old who is crawling and pulling up cannot be left alone in a room while awake. So every waking hour of hers is a working hour of somebody's. At this age she sleeps roughly 12–16 hours in 24 including naps, which leaves about 12 waking hours a day that must be covered — 84 hours a week. On top of that: cooking her food from scratch (you are vegetarian, so almost nothing comes out of a jar), the family's meals, dishes, the daily infant laundry, cleaning, groceries, and the night wakings. Call the whole thing 100 to 110 hours a week, with no days off and no lunch break.

Your side: nine hours in the office plus about two hours of commuting, five days. Call it 55 hours, with a chair, a lunch, adult conversation, and a door you walk out of. Both of you are working. Only one of you is off duty at 7pm and on Saturday. That gap — not effort, not love, not intention — is the entire problem. Nothing on this page works if you do not accept the gap first.

Why “helping more” will not close it. Helping means she still owns the job and delegates pieces of it to you. She still has to notice the nappies are running out, decide what the baby eats on Tuesday, remember the vaccine is due, and then ask you. The noticing and deciding is the work. It is the part that does not stop when she sits down. You cannot fix a 105-hour week by doing more tasks. You fix it by taking whole domains off her desk — she never thinks about them again — and by buying hours with money.

The thing you are avoiding. You have a Dubai plan. If that plan lands, she moves again — to a third country, further from family, with a toddler. If you carry this arrangement across, you will import the problem at a higher rent. The childcare-and-support plan is not something to sort out after the offer. It goes in the same spreadsheet as the salary target, and it goes in now.

The three moves, in order of leverage

Move 1 · costs money

Buy hours back

The single highest-leverage action on this page, and the only one that works while you are physically at the office. Two hires, both part-time, both routine in KL:

  • A part-time cleaner, twice a week, ~4 hours each. Removes cleaning, floors, bathrooms and the laundry mountain permanently.
  • A babysitter, twice a week, ~3 hours, so she can leave the house alone in daylight — not to run errands. To sit somewhere and be a person.

Likely cost: KL listing platforms in 2026 quote part-time helpers at roughly RM15–25/hour and babysitters at roughly RM15–35/hour. At RM25/hour, the 14 hours a week above is ~RM350/week, ~RM1,500/month. Get three actual quotes before you trust that number.

Assumed: RM1,500/month is under 10% of household income. If that is wrong, cut the babysitter first and keep the cleaner — but do not cut both, and do not use “we can manage” as the reason. Managing is what she is already doing.

Move 2 · costs ego

Own domains, not tasks

A domain means: you own the outcome, the noticing, the restocking, and the decision. She is never the fallback. Take these six, whole, starting this week:

  • Bedtime. Every night. Yours. See §06 — this one is load-bearing.
  • All of her food prep. Sunday batch cook, weekday assembly. See §05.
  • Medical. Vaccines, appointments, the KKM health book, the paediatrician's number.
  • Groceries and household stock. Nappies, wipes, formula, rice, dal. You track it.
  • Dishes, bottles and sterilising. All of them, every night. Non-negotiable.
  • Admin. Bills, insurance, visas, school waitlists, the passport.

Rule: if she has to remind you about anything in this list, you do not own it yet. You are still being helped.

Move 3 · costs nothing

The Off-Duty Block

Four continuous hours, twice each weekend, where she is not responsible for the baby and — ideally — not in the house. Not “go rest, I've got her” while she folds laundry in the next room. Out. Phone on silent.

Continuous is the whole point. Six separate twenty-minute breaks do not add up to two hours of rest; they add up to six interruptions. Fragmented time does not restore anyone.

This is also the cheapest protective factor you have available. Poor social support is a repeatedly identified risk factor for perinatal depressive symptoms — and in this house, you are the social support. There is nobody else.

The one-line test. At the end of any given week, ask: did she get eight continuous off-duty hours, and did she have to ask for any of it? Eight hours she had to negotiate for is a worse week than six she did not. If the answer is no two weeks running, stop reading the rest of this page and fix that. Everything else here is optimisation on top of a system that is not yet solvent.

The handover, when you walk in the door

The first ninety seconds of your evening set the next four hours. Do this, in this order, every weekday. It takes under two minutes and it is the highest-return two minutes in the day.

  • Put the phone down first. Before the door, not after the greeting. She will notice which came first and she will be right to.
  • Greet your wife before the baby. One sentence, eye contact, actual contact. The baby is delighted to see you either way; your wife has been waiting since 8am for an adult.
  • Ask one specific question, never “how was your day.” Try: “What was the worst twenty minutes?” It is answerable, and it gives her permission to say something true.
  • Take the baby physically, immediately. Not in ten minutes. Not after you change. Hands out, take her, walk away with her.
  • Say the handover line out loud: “I've got her until she's asleep.” Then actually have her until she's asleep.
03 — The weekday

Monday to Friday, hour by hour

Built around a 9–6 office and a round-trip commute of about two hours. Shift every time by the same offset if yours differs; the order is what matters, not the clock. Blocks marked YOU are yours and do not get renegotiated on a bad day — that is the whole point of writing them down.

The design principle: she should be off duty from the moment you walk in until the moment the baby is asleep. Everything below is arranged to make that true even on the days you are wrecked.

Say goodbye to the baby out loud, cheerfully, even though she may cry. Do not sneak out. Sneaking teaches her that people vanish without warning, which makes separation worse, not better. A short, cheerful, identical goodbye every day teaches her that you leave and you come back.

Before you leave: text your wife one line about what you have already handled today. She should not have to discover it.

While you are at the office

You are not present, but you are not absent either. Four rules, all of which cost under a minute:

  • One text at ~11am that is not logistics. Not “did she nap”. Something for your wife, about your wife. The logistics text can come separately.
  • Order things during the day. Nappies, wipes, groceries, that 9–12m clothing. You have a desk and a browser; she has a baby pulling on her leg. Ordering is a job that can be done from an office and it should never be hers.
  • Take the call. If she calls in the middle of the day, she has weighed it against interrupting you. Answer it or call back within ten minutes.
  • Leave when you said you would. Every unannounced hour of overtime is an hour she does solo, past the point she had budgeted for. If you will be late, tell her by 5pm, not 6:30.

You are both sleep-deprived. Staying up to reclaim the evening is the most common and most expensive mistake in this phase — it borrows tomorrow's patience to buy tonight's freedom. Some nights, take the loan knowingly. Not most nights.

Nights

The default that fails: whoever the baby will accept goes, which becomes your wife every time, which means she has not slept unbroken since December. Fix it structurally, not heroically.

  • You take everything before midnight. Every waking, every night, weekdays included. You are awake anyway.
  • She takes the after-midnight wakings on work nights — but only until the baby will accept you at 3am, which is the point of you owning bedtime. Once she will, split the night in half properly.
  • You take the whole night on Friday and Saturday. Non-negotiable. Two nights a week where she goes to bed knowing she will not be woken is the difference between coping and not.
  • If she is still breastfeeding at night, you still get up: you do the nappy, the settle and the return to cot. She feeds and goes straight back to sleep. The feed is the short part; the settling is the long part.
Honest caveat about this routine. No week will run like this. Teething, illness, work travel and bad nights will break it — and on those days the routine's job is not to be followed, it is to tell you what to restore first. Priority order when the day collapses: bedtime, then the kitchen close-down, then the Golden 90. Drop everything else guilt-free.

The Sunday Twenty — fixed agenda

Same five questions every week, in this order. Timer on. When it rings, you stop, even mid-sentence — an unfinished item goes to next Sunday, and that constraint is what stops it becoming a two-hour grievance session.

05 — Food

Feeding a vegetarian eight-month-old, honestly

This is the section with a real downside if it is wrong, so everything below is either sourced or explicitly flagged as a judgement call. Read the risk box first. It is the part most Indian vegetarian families get wrong, and it is fixable in a week.

The risk, stated plainly. Iron is the number-one nutritional problem for a vegetarian infant, and it is not a small one — iron deficiency in infancy is associated with learning difficulties, not just tiredness. The CDC states it directly: plant (non-heme) iron is less easily absorbed than animal (heme) iron, and “getting your baby enough iron from non-heme iron sources alone will require careful planning.” You do not eat meat, so you are in the careful-planning case by definition. Planning here means the four rules below, not good intentions.

The four iron rules

Rule 1

Iron-fortified infant cereal, most days

The CDC lists it first among non-heme sources, and it is the most reliable one because the dose is on the label rather than in your estimate. Widely stocked in KL supermarkets and pharmacies.

Many Indian families skip it on principle — homemade is better, shop-bought is lazy. On iron, that instinct is backwards. Homemade ragi and dal are good food; fortified cereal is the one item where you know the number. Use both.

Rule 2

Pair every iron meal with vitamin C

Vitamin C markedly improves absorption of plant iron. The CDC's own list: citrus, broccoli, cabbage, berries, papaya, tomatoes, sweet potato.

In practice, in your kitchen: a squeeze of lemon into the dal after cooking; tomato in the khichdi; mashed papaya or steamed sweet potato served alongside. Cheap, invisible, and it does more than swapping the lentil.

Rule 3

Keep big dairy away from the iron meal

Calcium competes with non-heme iron for absorption. So the large milk feed and the iron-dense meal should not be the same event.

Practical version: solids first, milk feed about an hour later. A spoon of curd with lunch is fine — a full bottle of milk with the dal is the pattern to avoid. This costs nothing and most people never hear it.

Rule 4

Test your wife's B12, not just the baby's

Plants do not make vitamin B12. A breastfed baby's B12 comes from the mother's stores — so if your wife has been vegetarian for years and is not supplementing, the baby's supply is her supply. Long-term vegetarians frequently run low without symptoms.

Action: ask the GP for a maternal B12 (and ferritin) test at the next visit. This is a blood test, not a debate. If it is low, it is corrected with a supplement in weeks.

Do not start any supplement on the strength of this page. Doses for infants depend on weight, milk intake and blood results, and I am not in a position to set one. Take these four rules to the paediatrician at the 9-month visit and ask the direct question: “She is vegetarian and breastfed. Do you want to check iron and B12, and does she need a supplement?” That sentence gets you a better answer than an hour of searching. Anaemia screening is routinely done around 12 months — you can ask for it earlier given the diet.

The do-not list — this one is not negotiable

Before her first birthday

Absolutely not, until 25 Dec 2026

  • Honey. Any amount, in any form — food, water, on a dummy. It can cause infant botulism, which is severe. This is the single most-broken rule in Indian households, usually with the best intentions.
  • Cow's milk as a drink. Risk of intestinal bleeding, and too much protein and mineral load for her kidneys. Dairy in food — yogurt, paneer, cheese, milk cooked into porridge — is fine from six months.
  • Fruit juice. None at all before twelve months. Whole fruit instead, always.
  • Added salt, stock cubes, bouillon. Her kidneys cannot handle it. Cook her portion out before you salt the family's.
  • Added sugar and jaggery. Including in her porridge, including “just a little for taste”. She has no requirement for it and it costs her teeth and her palate.
  • Tea and coffee. No caffeine under two. Tannins also block iron absorption — a double loss in a vegetarian diet.
  • Unpasteurised milk, yogurt or cheese. And no mould-ripened or blue cheeses.
Choking

Form matters as much as food

  • Whole nuts: not until five years. Ground or as a thin smear of nut butter on something — never a spoonful of nut butter on its own.
  • Whole grapes and cherry tomatoes: quarter them lengthways. Every time.
  • Raw carrot, raw apple, hard raw vegetables: steam until they squash between your finger and thumb.
  • Raw jelly cubes: a choking hazard in themselves.
  • Popcorn, boiled sweets, chunks of hard cheese, whole cherry-sized anything.
  • Rice drinks as a milk substitute, under five — arsenic. Eating rice is fine.

The two positive rules: she eats sitting upright, in a highchair, never in a car seat, never lying back, never walking around. And an adult stays with her the entire meal — in the room, looking at her, not in the kitchen.

Learn infant choking first aid. Both of you, this month. Back blows and chest thrusts for an infant are different from the adult manoeuvre. A twenty-minute video is not the same as a class; do the class.

Allergens

Current guidance is to introduce common allergens early rather than delay them, one at a time, in small amounts, at home, earlier in the day so you have hours to observe. For your household the relevant ones are peanut (as smooth butter thinned into food), tree nuts (ground), sesame (as tahini or ground til), wheat, soy and dairy.

One new food every three days, nothing new at dinner, nothing new on a day she is unwell or being vaccinated. Watch for hives, swelling around the mouth or eyes, vomiting, or wheeze.

If there is a family history of food allergy, eczema or asthma — talk to the doctor before the first introduction, not after. That is the one case where you do not just try it at home.

Water, milk and cups

  • Water: small amounts with meals, from an open or straw cup, from six months. Boiled and cooled, or filtered — this is KL, use what you already drink.
  • Start the open cup now. Not a bottle, not a sippy with a valve. She will spill most of it for a month and then she will be able to drink from a cup, which is a real skill.
  • Milk feeds stay the backbone until twelve months. Solids are practice and iron; they are not yet replacing milk.
  • No bottle in the cot, ever. Pooled milk against teeth overnight causes decay, and it builds a sleep association you will spend months undoing.
  • Teeth: once any tooth is through, brush twice a day with a smear of fluoride toothpaste. Start now and it is normal; start at two and it is a war.

The Sunday batch cook — your two hours

One session, whole week done. This is the block that makes every other weekday possible.

  • 1. Cook three bases, big. A moong dal khichdi, a masoor dal, and one plain grain (rice or ragi). No salt in any of them.
  • 2. Steam a tray of vegetables. Carrot, sweet potato, pumpkin, beans, broccoli. Some mashed, some left as batons for self-feeding.
  • 3. Portion into an ice-cube tray or 60–100 ml pots. Cube-sized portions are the trick: you thaw exactly what you need instead of a whole day's worth.
  • 4. Freeze flat, label with the date. Use within about a month. Rotate front to back.
  • 5. Reheat once, thoroughly, and never refreeze. Reheat until piping hot, then cool to lukewarm before serving. Test on your wrist, not your finger.
  • 6. Never re-serve from a bowl she has eaten out of. Saliva in the bowl means the leftovers go in the bin, not the fridge. Serve from a portion, keep the rest untouched.

06 — Sleep

Getting her down, every night, by you

Sleep is where you can take the most weight off your wife for the least skill. It is also the area with the most confident nonsense written about it, so the sourced parts and the judgement parts are marked separately below.

Why bedtime must be yours and not shared. If you only do bedtime when your wife is exhausted, the baby learns that mum settles her and you are the substitute. Then at 3am she will accept only mum — and your wife can never be off duty, no matter how willing you are. Owning bedtime every night is not a chore you are taking on. It is the mechanism that makes her sleep possible. Expect three to four hard weeks. Pay them.

The bedtime sequence — seven steps, same order, every night

The evidence for a consistent bedtime routine is good: it helps children fall asleep faster and reduces night wakings. The specific steps matter far less than the fact that they never change. Pick these, then do not improvise.

1
19:30 — Lights down, house down

Overheads off, one lamp. TV off. Voices drop. The environment change is the first cue and it starts before she is in the room.

2
19:35 — Bath

Ten minutes, warm, unhurried. On nights you skip the bath, still do a warm wipe-down so the step exists. Skipping steps entirely is what breaks the sequence.

3
19:45 — Massage and dress, on the same mat, in the same room

Slow hands. Talk quietly the whole time. Sleepsuit, nappy, sleeping bag if you use one.

4
19:55 — Milk feed, dim light, no screens in the room

Yours to give if bottle-fed. If your wife is feeding, she feeds and hands her straight back — the feed is not the end of the sequence, and it should not be the thing that puts her to sleep.

5
20:05 — Two books, always the same two at bedtime

The predictability is the point at bedtime; keep the variety for daytime reading (§07). Quiet voice, slow pace, no lifting-flap excitement.

6
20:12 — The same short song, then the same sentence

Pick one line and never change the wording: “Goodnight, I love you, I'll see you in the morning.” Then into the cot.

7
20:15 — Into the cot awake, and you leave

Drowsy but awake. If she only ever arrives in the cot already asleep, she wakes at 1am somewhere unfamiliar and calls for the thing that was there when she went under — you, or a feed. Putting her down awake is what teaches her the cot is where sleep happens.

The sleep environment

Safe sleep — non-negotiable

What goes in the cot: her

  • On her back to sleep, every sleep. Once she rolls herself over in the night you do not need to keep flipping her back — but you always put her down on her back.
  • Firm, flat mattress. Fitted sheet. Nothing else. No pillows, no duvet, no bumpers, no soft toys, no positioners. Soft items in the cot are a SIDS risk factor.
  • Not too warm. KL is hot; the instinct to over-dress at night is the wrong one. Feel the back of her neck, not her hands — hands run cool and mislead you.
  • Cot clear of cords, blind pulls and anything she can pull in. She can pull to standing now, so re-check the reachable radius at cot-rail height.
Conditions

Dark, dull and boring

  • Dark. Blackout the room. A dim red-toned nightlight if you need one to see by.
  • White noise, steady, not loud, on all night rather than switched off once she is down — so a 2am noise does not stand out against silence.
  • Cool as you can get it. Fan or air-con; keep the airflow off her directly.
  • Daylight in the morning, deliberately. Get her outside or by a window early. Light exposure in the day is what anchors her body clock to the actual day/night cycle — and it is also the easiest thing to skip in a hot city.

Night wakings — the ladder

Climb it one rung at a time. Most wakings are resolved on rungs one to three, and going straight to rung five every time is how a two-minute wake becomes a ninety-minute one.

  • 1. Wait two minutes. Actually look at a clock — two minutes feels like ten at 3am. Babies cycle and resettle constantly. A great many wakings end here if you do not intervene.
  • 2. Listen to the cry. Grizzling and complaining is different from distress. Only the second one needs you immediately.
  • 3. Go in, but do not lift. Hand on the chest, same quiet phrase, low light. See if she goes down from there.
  • 4. Lift, settle, put back down still awake. Not asleep in your arms and transferred — that is rung six with extra steps.
  • 5. Check the obvious. Nappy, too hot, teething, hungry, unwell. Feed if she is genuinely hungry; she is eight months old, not being manipulative.
  • 6. If it goes past an hour, or she is inconsolable and not usually like this — that is different from a normal waking. Temperature, ears, rash. See §12.

The eight-month regression, honestly

What is true: around this age several things land at once — teething, a sharp rise in separation anxiety, new motor skills she wants to practise at 2am, and much more awareness of her surroundings. Any of those disrupts sleep, and they often arrive together.

What is oversold: “the 8-month regression” as a fixed, universal event on a schedule. Sleep Foundation is explicit that not all babies experience it and that infant sleep patterns vary widely. It is a useful label for a real cluster of causes, not a diagnosis, and it is not a thing you have to wait out passively.

What to do: hold the routine rather than inventing new ones. A regression is exactly when people start bringing the baby into their bed, feeding to sleep, or driving her around the block — and each of those becomes a new habit that outlives the regression by months. Change nothing, offer more comfort inside the existing structure, wait it out.

Separation anxiety, and the mistake that makes it worse

  • Never sneak out. Not at bedtime, not when you leave for work. A cheerful, identical, two-second goodbye every time teaches her that departures are announced and survivable. Vanishing teaches her to monitor you constantly.
  • Practise short separations in daylight. Leave the room, call out from the next room, come back. Thirty seconds, then a minute. You are building the evidence base that you return.
  • Say “Daddy's back!” on the way in. Announce returns as clearly as departures.
  • Peek-a-boo is not just a game — it is object permanence practice, which is the exact cognitive skill underneath separation anxiety. Play it constantly right now.
  • Do not lift her out of the cot for crying that is separation protest rather than distress. Comfort her in the cot. Lifting teaches that crying reverses bedtime.

Sleep training — this is a values decision, not a technical one

You will get absolute confidence from both directions on this. Here is the fork, stated without me picking for you, because this one is genuinely yours and your wife's to choose together:

  • No formal training. Respond every time; treat night waking as developmental and wait it out. Costs the most parental sleep. Chosen by many families with no ill effect.
  • Gradual / fading. You stay in the room and reduce your involvement over one to two weeks — sitting by the cot, then further, then out. Slowest and gentlest. Requires consistency more than nerve.
  • Timed checks. Leave, return at set and lengthening intervals, comfort briefly without lifting. Usually the fastest of the structured methods.

The two things that are true whichever you pick: the method matters far less than doing the same thing every night for at least two weeks; and if you and your wife pick differently, the baby gets neither. Decide together, out loud, on a Sunday — not at 2am in the dark. That is when this argument actually happens, and it is the worst possible time to have it.

07 — Books

How to actually build a reader

You asked how to teach her to read and how to make her understand. The honest answer is that at eight months you are not teaching reading at all — you are building the thing reading is made of, which is spoken language. Get that right now and reading arrives almost on its own at five or six.

The one idea to take from this section. The books are not the active ingredient. Your voice is. A child who is talked to and read with — questions, pauses, back-and-forth — builds vocabulary faster than one who is read at. The technique below has a name and an evidence base, and it is free.

The method: dialogic reading

Ordinary shared reading is: adult reads, child listens. Dialogic reading inverts it — you gradually make the child the teller of the story and you become the listener. It is the best-evidenced shared-reading practice there is for boosting oral vocabulary and expressive language.

Honest scope note: the technique proper is designed for roughly two- to five-year-olds. At eight months you run the infant version below, and you switch to the real thing around eighteen months to two years. Knowing where it is going tells you what to build now.

PEER — the four-move loop, used on a single page

  • P — Prompt. Ask about something on the page. “What's that?”
  • E — Evaluate. Respond to her answer. “That's right!”
  • E — Expand. Add to it. “Yes — a big brown dog.”
  • R — Repeat. Ask it back so she uses the longer version. “Can you say big brown dog?”

CROWD — the five kinds of prompt, in rising difficulty

  • C — Completion. Leave the last word of a rhyming line for her to fill in.
  • R — Recall. “What happened to the little bear?”
  • O — Open-ended. “Tell me what's happening on this page.”
  • W — Wh-. What, where, who, why questions about the picture.
  • D — Distancing. Link the book to her life: “We saw a dog like that at the park, didn't we?” — the hardest and the most valuable.

10 — Being a husband

The marriage is the thing most likely to be quietly damaged this year

You asked how to be a loving husband at the same time. The honest answer is that “at the same time” is the wrong frame — most of being a good husband this year is the childcare. But not all of it, and the rest is below.

The diagnosis. Right now she is a full-time carer in a country where she has no mother, no sister, no friend who can take the baby for an hour. Her entire adult social world is one person, and that person is out of the house from 8 to 7. That is not a marriage under strain because of a baby — it is one adult carrying an unshared load and slowly running out of self. Affection does not fix that. Structure does. Sections 03 and 04 are the actual love letter; this section is what goes on top.

The thing to watch for, stated without softening

Malaysian studies put postnatal depression at around 14% of mothers, with estimates across studies ranging from roughly 7% to 27% depending on method and population. Poor social support is one of the consistently identified risk factors — and her social support, structurally, is you. Depression that begins after birth frequently persists well past the first few months, so “she's eight months in, that window has passed” is not reassurance.

What to actually look for

  • Flatness rather than sadness — nothing is enjoyable, including things that used to be
  • Withdrawal: not calling home, not messaging friends, declining every invitation
  • Anxiety that will not switch off, or a constant sense that something is wrong with the baby
  • Not sleeping even when the baby sleeps
  • Irritability and anger — depression in exhausted people very often presents as rage, not tears
  • Saying she is a bad mother, or that the baby would be better off without her
  • Eating badly or not at all, and losing interest in how she looks in a way that is new

What you do: say it out loud and kindly — “I think you are having a much harder time than you are telling me, and I want us to see someone.” Then book the appointment yourself and go with her. A GP or a Klinik Kesihatan can screen for this routinely; the standard screening instrument is a short questionnaire, not an ordeal. Do not wait for her to raise it. The illness is the thing that stops people raising it.

If she ever says anything about harming herself or the baby, that is not a Sunday conversation — get help the same day.

The five daily acts

All five cost under twenty minutes combined. Do all five every day and the routine sections become a marriage rather than a shift rota.

  • 1. Ask a real question and hear the answer. Not “how was your day”. Try: “What was the worst twenty minutes?” or “What did she do that you wanted to tell someone about?” Then listen without solving. She is not raising a problem for you to close; she is trying to be known.
  • 2. Name one specific thing she did. Not “thanks for everything” — that is noise. “You got her to eat the whole bowl of dal today. I couldn't have.” Specific praise says I was paying attention, which is the actual message.
  • 3. Touch her once, non-sexually, with no agenda. A hand on the back, a hug in the kitchen, sitting close. Touch that never leads anywhere is what makes touch that does lead somewhere welcome instead of another demand.
  • 4. Take something off her list before she asks. Anything. Every day. The unprompted part is the whole value.
  • 5. Say something that is about her, not about the mother. She has been Amma all day. Ask about the book she is reading, the thing she used to be good at, the argument she had with her sister. Ten minutes a day of being treated as a person is the cheapest antidepressant available.

The repair script

You will get this wrong regularly. Repair matters more than avoidance — couples do not survive by never fighting, they survive by repairing fast. Four moves, in order, and skipping straight to the fourth is the usual mistake:

  • 1. Name it without qualifying. “I checked my phone while you were talking. That was dismissive.” No “but”. The word but deletes everything before it.
  • 2. Say what it cost her, not what you meant. Your intention is not the injury.
  • 3. Say the specific change. “Phone goes in the drawer at seven.” Not “I'll do better.”
  • 4. Then do it, visibly, for long enough that she notices without being told. That last part is the apology. The first three were the preamble.

And when she is angry about something that is genuinely not your fault: she is not filing a claim, she is at the end of a 105-hour week. “That sounds awful, I'm sorry” costs you nothing and is true. Defending yourself against an accusation she has not actually made is how a bad day becomes a bad fortnight.

Intimacy, honestly

Nobody writes this part down, so it festers. Plainly:

  • Exhaustion is not rejection, and eight months in it is still the most likely explanation by a wide margin.
  • Being touched all day changes what touch means. A person who has had a baby on her body for twelve hours may want physical space, not more contact. That is not about you.
  • The route back is almost always non-sexual affection first, and rest. Point 3 of the five daily acts is not a tactic — but it is also the mechanism.
  • Talk about it in daylight, kindly, once. Not at night, not as a complaint, not as a tally. “I miss you. Not as a demand — I just want you to know I do.”
  • If it has been a long time and neither of you will say so, say so. Silence around this reliably becomes resentment on both sides, each convinced it is the other's doing.

The item nobody raises: her career

This is the most consequential thing in this section and the most likely to be skipped. A woman who leaves work to raise a child in a foreign country, with no support network, does not pause her career — she starts losing it. The gap compounds: skills, network, confidence, and the story she can tell an interviewer. By the time the child is three, the re-entry is materially harder than it would have been at one.

You are the person in this house who runs campaigns to get jobs. You have a whole site for it. Turn some of that on her.

  • Ask her, properly, once a quarter: does she want to go back, and to what? Not as a hint. As a genuine question with time to answer it, and with the assumption that the answer may change.
  • If yes: the plan starts now, not at three. That means budgeted childcare, a live CV and LinkedIn, a course, and one protected hour a week that is hers for skills. One hour. Put it in the calendar like a meeting.
  • If no, or not yet: say that is a real choice and mean it — and then make sure she has something of her own anyway. Not a hobby you assigned. Something she picked.
  • Whichever it is, do not let her financial position depend on your goodwill. Joint visibility on everything, her own account, her own savings. This is not about trust; it is about her not being someone whose options narrowed while she was busy.
The Dubai problem — the honest attack on your own plan. Your stated priority is a Controller or Head of FP&A role in the UAE by mid-2026, and that plan currently has a hole in it. If you land it, your wife moves to a third country — further from family, on a dependent visa, with a toddler, having to rebuild a support network from nothing for the second time in two years. If you carry the current arrangement across, you will be paying a much larger rent for the same isolation. The support plan is part of the offer evaluation, not something to sort out afterwards: what is childcare there, what will you actually pay for it, what is her visa and work status, and can she work at all? Price those before you negotiate salary, because they change what a number is worth. A package that looks better and leaves her more alone is a worse package.

11 — Tips & tricks

The operational list

Small, specific, mostly learned the hard way. None of these is important on its own; together they are the difference between a household that runs and one that is always firefighting.

Systems

Make the house do the remembering

  • Two of everything, in two places. Nappies, wipes, spare clothes, muslins — one set in her room, one by the door. The trip across the flat with a naked baby is the trip you will regret.
  • The bag never gets unpacked, only restocked. Restock it at the kitchen close-down, not on the way out. Keep a laminated card of contents inside it.
  • Buy on a subscription, not on noticing. Nappies, wipes, formula. Running out is a failure of process, and the process is yours now.
  • One shared calendar, and it is the truth. Vaccines, appointments, your late nights, her blocks. If it is not in there it does not exist.
  • A whiteboard on the fridge with today's feeds, naps and anything unusual. It ends the handover interrogation and it is genuinely useful when a doctor asks.
  • Clothes one size up, bought early. She will outgrow things overnight and you will discover it at 6:45am.
  • Keep a stocked medicine box — infant paracetamol, a digital thermometer, oral rehydration salts, saline drops, barrier cream. Check the dates twice a year. Ask the pharmacist for the correct dose for her weight and write it on the box.
Crying and settling

The order to try things in

  • Run the checklist before you improvise: hungry, wind, nappy, too hot or cold, tired, overstimulated, teething, bored, unwell. Most crying is on that list.
  • Change the environment, not the technique. Take her outside. Nine times in ten, walking out of the front door resets a crying baby faster than anything you do indoors.
  • Water works. Bath, shower, running tap. An unscheduled bath at 5pm is a legitimate tool, not a failure.
  • Your voice low and slow beats loud and soothing. Drop your pitch and your pace; she matches you.
  • Wear her. A carrier buys you two hands and she sleeps. Learn to put it on alone, properly, before you need it.
  • The witching hour is real. Late afternoon is hard for a lot of babies. Plan the day so that 5–7pm has no ambitions in it.
  • When you are past it — put her down safely in the cot and walk out for two minutes. A baby crying alone in a cot for two minutes is safe. A parent who has stopped coping while holding her is not. Never shake a baby, ever, and never let anyone else.
Teething

Blamed for everything, cause of some of it

  • Cold, damp washcloth to chew. A chilled — not frozen — teether.
  • Clean finger, firm gum massage. She will bite. Let her.
  • Extra drooling means a rash on the chin: barrier cream, and change the bib often.
  • Avoid amber necklaces — strangulation and choking risk with no established benefit. Also avoid teething gels containing sugar or unlabelled ingredients.
  • Teething does not cause a high fever. If she has a real fever, look for a real cause — see §12. This is the single most common misattribution and it delays diagnoses.
Illness

The first sick week

  • Fluids beat food. A day of not eating is survivable; a day of not drinking is not. Milk feeds count.
  • Write down times and temperatures. By hour eighteen you will not remember, and the doctor's first question will be exactly that.
  • One parent sleeps properly. Split the night formally instead of both of you being half-awake — you will need a functioning adult tomorrow.
  • Do not restart sleep training during illness. Comfort freely; return to the routine when she is well. You are not undoing months of work.
  • Expect a sleep and appetite hangover for about a week after she recovers.
Out of the house

Restaurants, malls, cars

  • Go early. First sitting, before the crowd and before she is tired. An 11:30 lunch out is easy; a 1:30 one is a fight.
  • Feed her first, then go. Do not rely on the restaurant to solve the meal.
  • Ask for the bill when the food arrives. You may need to leave in ninety seconds and you do not want to be waving at anyone.
  • KL malls are the underrated infrastructure — air-conditioned, flat, with nursing rooms and lifts. On a bad-weather day, a mall lap is a legitimate outing.
  • Car seat every single time, no exceptions, including the five-minute trip. Rear facing as long as the seat allows.
  • Never leave her in a parked car. In this climate a car interior becomes lethal in minutes, not hours.
Flying and travel

For the India trips

  • Feed or offer a cup on take-off and descent — swallowing sorts out her ears.
  • Book the bassinet row the moment you book the ticket, and reconfirm at check-in.
  • Twice the nappies you think, in the cabin. Delays happen; hold luggage does not help.
  • A change of clothes for you as well as her. Learned once, never forgotten.
  • Take the routine with you — same sleeping bag, same two bedtime books, same song. The portable cues are what let her sleep in an unfamiliar room.
  • Do not expect the schedule to survive. Give it three days back home to reset and do not conclude anything about her sleep from a trip.
Family and advice

The politics

  • You handle your family; she handles hers. Never make her deliver a difficult message to your parents. This one rule prevents a large fraction of all in-law conflict.
  • “That's interesting, we'll think about it” ends any unwanted advice without a fight. Use it liberally and then do what you decided.
  • Some old advice is dangerous, not just outdated: honey before one, rice water instead of milk, sleeping on the front, water for a young infant, gripe waters of unknown composition. Be polite and be immovable.
  • Visitors are a cost unless they cook or clean. A guest who holds the baby for an hour and then expects tea has made the day harder. It is fine to say so, and it is your job to say it rather than hers.
  • Praise your wife in front of your family, specifically. It changes how they treat her when you are not in the room.
You

The father, honestly

  • You are allowed to find this hard and boring in places. Saying so out loud is not disloyalty; pretending otherwise is what makes men withdraw.
  • Fathers get postnatal depression too. If you are numb, angry, drinking more, or working late to avoid coming home — that last one especially — take it seriously.
  • Do not let your entire self become work and baby. One thing a week that is yours, and grant it to her without keeping score.
  • Keep one friend you tell the truth to. Most men lose this in the first year and do not notice until much later.
  • Your daughter does not need you to be impressive. She needs you to be there, the same way, repeatedly, in a way she can predict. That is the whole job.
